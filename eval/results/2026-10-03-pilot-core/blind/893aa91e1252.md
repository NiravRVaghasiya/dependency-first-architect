# Plan: Real-Time Fraud Detection for Card Transactions

## 0. Assumptions (please check these)

- **Who we are:** a card issuer or processor scoring authorizations inline. If you're a merchant or acquirer, the design is mostly the same, but the actions and the source of labels change.
- **Volume:** about 2–5k transactions per second (TPS) on average and about 20k TPS at peak. Card-present and card-not-present (CNP) are mixed.
- **Latency:** the network gives us roughly 100–300 ms end to end for an authorization. Fraud scoring gets **≤ 50 ms at p99**.
- **Success metrics:** fraud losses in basis points (bps) of volume, false-positive ratio (good transactions declined per fraud caught), customer friction rate, and latency.

---

## 1. Architecture

```
                 ┌──────────────── SYNCHRONOUS (auth path, ≤50ms) ────────────────┐
Card network ─► Auth gateway ─► Scoring service ─► Decision engine ─► Approve / Decline / Step-up (3DS/OTP)
 (ISO 8583)          │             │     ▲               │
                     │             │     │ online features (Redis/Aerospike, <5ms)
                     │             ▼     │               │
                     │        Model server (GBDT, in-process or gRPC)
                     ▼                                    ▼
              ┌──────────── Kafka: auth events, decisions, scores ────────────┐
              ▼                          ▼                                    ▼
   Stream processor (Flink)     Async detectors                   Lakehouse (Iceberg/Delta)
   - velocity/aggregate          - graph/ring detection            - raw events, features, decisions
     features → online store     - post-auth anomaly alerts        - labels (chargebacks, confirmations)
                                 - case creation                   ▼
                                         ▼                    Training / backtesting / monitoring
                                 Case mgmt + analyst UI ──► labels ──┘
```

There are two paths:
- **Inline (synchronous):** must be fast and simple, and must keep working when parts of it fail.
- **Async:** can run heavier analysis, such as graph checks, sequence models and fraud-ring detection. It produces alerts, blocks on cards, and features that the inline path uses on later transactions.

## 2. Components

### 2.1 Ingestion
- Parse the authorization message into one canonical event schema. Use a schema registry (Avro/Protobuf).
- **PCI DSS:** tokenize the card number (PAN) at the edge and never store CVV or track data. Everything after the edge uses tokens. This keeps most of the system out of PCI audit scope.
- Write every authorization, score, decision and reason code to Kafka. This is the record of what happened, used for audit, training and replay.

### 2.2 Features

| Type | Examples | Where it's computed |
|---|---|---|
| **Transaction** | amount, merchant category code (MCC), entry mode (chip/swipe/CNP), currency, cross-border flag, local time | In the request |
| **Velocity / aggregates** | count and sum per card over 1m/1h/24h/7d; distinct merchants or countries per card in 24h; amount vs. the card's 30-day average (z-score) | Flink → online store |
| **Profile / behavioral** | card's usual MCCs, geography and hours; first time at this merchant; days since card issued or last address/phone change | Batch + stream |
| **Entity risk** | merchant fraud rate, BIN risk, device/IP/email reputation (CNP), how many cards share a device | Batch + stream |
| **Geo** | distance and speed since the last card-present transaction ("impossible travel") | Online store (last location per card) |
| **Graph (async)** | links between card, device, email, address and merchant; distance to known fraud | Async job → online store |

Key design points:
- **Include the current transaction in velocity counts.** Flink lags by seconds, so the scoring service should read the stored aggregates and add the current transaction itself, or atomically increment counters at score time. Otherwise card-testing bursts get through.
- **Avoid train/serve skew.** Define each feature once and use that definition for both streaming and batch backfill, via a feature platform like Feast or Tecton or a shared Flink job. Training data must be point-in-time correct, with no information from after the transaction.
- **Online store:** keyed by card token, merchant, device, etc., with TTLs. Target p99 under 5 ms and replicate across availability zones.

### 2.3 Models (layered)
1. **Rules engine.** Hard blocks (stolen-card lists, sanctioned regions), quick fixes for new attack patterns, and regulatory rules. Rules should be editable by analysts, versioned, and testable against past traffic before they go live.
2. **Main model: gradient-boosted trees (LightGBM/XGBoost).** These work well on tabular data, are fast (under 2 ms in-process), and SHAP values give per-decision reason codes. Train separate models, or one model with segment features, for card-present vs. CNP.
3. **Later: sequence models** (a transformer or GRU over each card's recent transactions) and graph neural networks (GNNs) in the async path. Their outputs feed the main model as features.
4. **Calibration** (isotonic or Platt) so scores are real probabilities. The decision engine needs that.

Training notes:
- Fraud is rare, often 0.05–0.2% of transactions. Downsample legitimate transactions and reweight, and judge models on precision-recall rather than accuracy.
- Measure **dollar-weighted recall** at a fixed false-positive rate, since catching a $2,000 fraud matters more than a $5 one.
- Validate on time-based splits only. Random splits leak future information.

### 2.4 Decision engine
Choose the action that costs the least, not the one that clears a fixed score cutoff:
- Expected cost of approving = p(fraud) × amount × loss rate
- Expected cost of declining = (1 − p) × (lost margin + cost of losing the customer)
- Step-up (3DS/OTP) is the middle option: a little friction, and it moves liability.

Thresholds are set per segment (product, region, MCC, customer tier) and kept in config, so they change without a redeploy. Every decision gets reason codes for customer service, the adverse-action notices some places require, and analyst review.

### 2.5 Labels and feedback (often the hardest part)
- **Sources:** chargebacks (arrive 30–120 days late), customer fraud reports, analyst decisions, and 3DS/OTP results.
- **Label maturity:** only train on transactions old enough for their labels to be mostly final. Use quicker signals, like customer confirmations, for early drift warnings.
- **Selection bias:** declined transactions never get a true label. Keep a small, controlled exploration slice (for example, step-up instead of decline on a random sample of borderline cases) so the model can be evaluated without bias.

## 3. Reliability and latency

- **Latency budget (p99):** parse 2 ms, feature fetch 5–10 ms, model 2–5 ms, rules and decision 2–5 ms, plus network. Total stays under 50 ms.
- **Fallbacks:** if features or the model time out, use rules only and a conservative default per segment (for example, approve below $X, step-up above). Never let the fraud system block authorizations, and use circuit breakers.
- **Deployment:** active-active across regions, with idempotent event handling (dedupe on transaction ID, since network retries are common).
- **Load testing:** at 2–3× peak, with a plan for holiday and Black Friday spikes and card-testing attacks.

---

## 4. Monitoring

- **System:** latency percentiles, timeout and fallback rate, Kafka consumer lag, feature freshness.
- **Model:** score distribution and feature drift (population stability index, PSI), decline and step-up rates by segment, alerts on sudden shifts.
- **Business:** fraud bps, false-positive ratio, analyst queue size and hit rate, chargeback rate.
- **Governance:** model registry, versioned features, rules and thresholds, documented validation (SR 11-7-style model risk management if you're a bank), fairness checks across regions and demographics where relevant.

---

## 5. Rollout phases

| Phase | Scope | Exit criteria |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Foundation** (≈6–8 wks) | Event schema, Kafka, tokenization, lakehouse, rules engine inline, labels pipeline | Rules live; ≥12 months of labeled history available |
| **2. Features + model, shadow mode** (≈6–8 wks) | Flink velocity features, online store, GBDT scoring logged but not acting | Matches offline results; p99 < 50 ms; no train/serve skew |
| **3. Go live, champion/challenger** (≈4 wks) | Model drives decisions on a growing traffic share; step-up flow; case management | Fraud bps ↓ and false positives flat or ↓ vs. control |
| **4. Advanced** (ongoing) | Graph/ring detection, sequence models, automated retraining, exploration slice | Gains measured per added part |

---

## 6. Main risks

- **Label delay and bias.** Mitigated by label maturity windows and the exploration slice.
- **Fraudsters adapting.** Mitigated by fast rule changes, frequent retraining, and drift alerts.
- **Train/serve skew.** Mitigated by one shared feature definition and shadow-mode comparison.
- **Too many false positives.** These lose customers. Mitigated by cost-based decisions and step-up instead of hard declines.
- **Fraud system outage blocking payments.** Mitigated by fallbacks and fail-safe defaults.

## Questions that would change the plan
1. Are you an issuer, acquirer/processor, or merchant? This decides the actions available, the labels, and the liability.
2. What are your real volume and latency limits, and what's the existing stack (cloud, Kafka/Flink already in use)?
3. Is the mix mostly card-present or CNP/e-commerce? CNP shifts weight toward device and identity signals.
4. How much labeled history do you have, and how good is it?
5. Do you have an in-house analyst team, or will a vendor (e.g., Featurespace, Feedzai, or Stripe Radar-type services) cover part of this? That's the build-vs-buy decision.
